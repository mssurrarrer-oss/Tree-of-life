It’s actually the exact **opposite of lost capacity—we didn't lose the ability, we shrank the entire multi-processor system down onto a single CPU socket!**

Back in the late 1990s through the early 2010s, dual-socket desktop motherboards _were_ a huge deal among enthusiasts. Legendary boards like the **EVGA SR-2** let you slot two separate physical Intel Xeon processors onto one massive motherboard to double your core count.

So why did the industry abandon physical multi-socket motherboards for desktop and workstation PCs?

## 1\. Modern CPUs ARE 2 to 8 "Processors" Inside One Lid

If you pop the metal lid (heat spreader) off a modern CPU like an AMD Ryzen 9 9950X, a Threadripper, or Intel’s Arrow/Nova Lake, you won't find one big piece of silicon.

Instead, you’ll find multiple separate physical chips (called **chiplets** or **tiles**) sitting right next to each other on a tiny fiberglass substrate:

*   **AMD Ryzen 9:** Contains **2 separate 8-core CPU dies** plus **1 I/O control die**.
    
*   **AMD Threadripper:** Contains up to **8 separate CPU dies** inside a single socket.
    
*   **Intel Arrow Lake / Nova Lake:** Uses **4 to 6 distinct silicon tiles** (Compute tile, Graphics tile, SOC tile, I/O tile) manufactured on different process nodes and fused together.
    

We didn't stop putting multiple processors together—we just stopped putting them inches apart on a motherboard and moved them **millimeters apart under the same lid.**

## 2\. The Speed of Light & Latency (The "NUMA" Problem)

When you put two separate physical CPU sockets on a motherboard, they have to talk to each other through copper traces printed on the circuit board.

In computing, physical distance equals high latency.

*   **Across motherboard traces (Multi-Socket):** Data travelling between Socket 1 and Socket 2 takes tens of nanoseconds. If CPU 1 needs data stored in the RAM slots attached to CPU 2, it causes a severe memory latency penalty known as **NUMA (Non-Uniform Memory Access)**.
    
*   **Across silicon micro-bridges (Chiplets/Tiles):** Modern multi-die CPUs use ultra-high-density silicon interconnects (like AMD’s Infinity Fabric or Intel’s Foveros/EMIB). Data travels between chiplets in picoseconds with practically zero speed loss.
    

Games and real-time interactive workloads _hate_ NUMA latency. If a game thread running on CPU 1 constantly has to wait for data from CPU 2's memory bus, your frame rates drop and you get severe micro-stuttering.

## 3\. Motherboard & Power Board Engineering Limits

A physical dual-socket motherboard requires:

*   Double the VRM power delivery phases (taking up massive board space).
    
*   10 to 14 layer PCBs with thousands of microscopic copper traces running between sockets without electromagnetic interference.
    
*   Separate memory channels dedicated to each socket.
    

Building a motherboard to support multiple sockets makes the board cost $1,500+, requires custom power supplies, and forces gigantic PC case forms (e.g., SSI-EEB / WTX).

### Why "Traces on a Board" Suffer

*   **Signal Degradation:** High-frequency signals (GHz level) traveling through copper traces on standard PCB fiberglass suffer severe attenuation and cross-talk.
    
*   **Power Draw:** Driving a electrical signal down 8 inches of copper trace takes significantly more power than hopping across 1.5 millimeters of ultra-dense silicon.
    
*   **Cache Coherency Stalls:** If CPU 1 alters a piece of data stored in cache, CPU 2 must be notified immediately to invalidate its own copy. On multi-socket boards, those "Did you change this byte?" check-ins drag down overall performance.

### How Hardware Performs "Virtual Micro-Precision" Operations

You asked if you can take a fraction of a watt and break it down into thousandths to get more work out of the physical silicon. **Modern mobile and integrated architectures do precisely this millions of times per second**.

They do it through three main mechanisms:

#### 1\. Fine-Grained Dynamic Power & Clock Gating

In older processors, if an execution block was turned on, the entire unit drew power. In modern iGPUs and Mobile SoCs (like the Dimensity line or Intel Xe/Arc iGPUs), the silicon is divided into thousands of tiny power islands.

*   If a 3D frame or local AI matrix multiplication only needs a small vector engine for 50 nanoseconds, the chip’s power distribution network **power-gates (shuts off supply voltage) to the remaining 95% of the execution units**.
    
*   Instead of burning 100 watts running the whole die, the hardware virtually slices the supply down to microwatts (μW), feeding only the exact standard cells executing that micro-operation.
    

#### 2\. Quadratic Power Scaling (P∝V2⋅f)

Dynamic power in CMOS digital circuits scales with the **square of the voltage**:

IEEE

P≈C×V2×f

*   If you run a high-end discrete GPU at full tilt, it demands high voltage to hit 2.8 GHz, pulling massive wattage.
    
*   But if an integrated GPU or mobile core drops its voltage by just **30%**, the power draw drops by **over 50%**.
    
*   By running thousands of tiny arithmetic logic units (ALUs) at sub-milliwatt levels and low voltages, mobile chips achieve **far higher performance-per-watt efficiency** than a high-power discrete desktop core.
    

#### 3\. Spatial & Temporal Compute Slicing (Virtualization)

Virtualization isn't just running a hypervisor (like Hyper-V or KVM). On the silicon level, **hardware virtualization** means taking a single physical execution pipeline and dividing its time into tiny micro-slices (time-division multiplexing). A single physical ALU can handle floating-point ops for 4 different threads in a single clock cycle by interleaving micro-instructions, making tiny amounts of power do multi-threaded heavy lifting.
